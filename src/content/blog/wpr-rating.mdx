I started playing padel in June. Somewhere along the way I got addicted to my rating. I kept playing with better and better players, and watching the number go up became half the fun. I care about challenging myself and getting better over time, and this sport gives me the perfect way to do it.

Then one evening in July I won 6-4 and my rating went up by 0.009. Basically nothing. I had won! So I went looking for how the World Padel Rating (the social one, WPR-s) actually works. WPR doesn't publish the formula, and I couldn't find anyone online who had worked it out. So I did, from about 7,000 real rating changes.

*Update, Oct 17: the first version of this post said only your team's average rating matters. I pulled ten times more data and that was wrong: your own rating counts more than your partner's. Point 3 is new, and every number on this page is updated.*

If you just play padel and want to know what moves your number, here's what I found.

## Five things to know about your WPR

**1. It counts games, not just wins.** About 80% of the update comes from your share of games won. Winning is a small bonus on top. In an even match, for someone with a few dozen matches who plays a few times a week:

| Score | Rating change |
|---|---|
| 6-0 | +0.13 |
| 6-2 | +0.08 |
| 6-4 | +0.05 |
| 7-6 | +0.03 |
| 6-7 | -0.03 |
| 4-6 | -0.05 |

So a 6-7 loss costs you less than a 6-4 win earns you. Every game matters, even when the set is already won. Here's an even set where you're up 5-1 and then switch off:

<WprTicker />

**2. Winning can lower your rating.** Before the match, the system works out how many games you should win, based on all four ratings. If your team is 1.0 higher, you need about 61% of the games just to break even. A 7-6 win is 54% and even a 6-4 is 60%, so both cost you a little. It works the other way too: lose 6-7 to a team 1.0 above you and you go up. Drag the slider and watch which scores flip:

<WprBreakEven />

**3. Your own rating counts more than your partner's.** I first thought only the team average mattered. It doesn't. What WPR expects from you is about 70% your own rating and 30% your partner's. So when partners have different ratings, the same result moves them differently: the stronger one gains less from a win and loses more from a loss. Here's a 3.6 and a 5.5 playing together against two players averaging 4.55:

| Score | 5.5 player | 3.6 player |
|---|---|---|
| Win 6-2 | +0.06 | +0.10 |
| Win 6-4 | +0.03 | +0.07 |
| Lose 4-6 | -0.07 | -0.03 |
| Lose 2-6 | -0.10 | -0.06 |

I've seen this happen to me. In October I won 6-4 with a partner rated 5.70. I went up 0.04, and my partner went down 0.01 for the same win.

**4. Best of three counts a lot more.** Winning 6-4 6-4 is worth about +0.08, against +0.05 for a single 6-4 set. If you're playing well, go for the longer match.

**5. New players move more, and gain more than they lose.** In your first match, a 6-4 win is worth about +0.22 and a 4-6 loss about -0.14. Both shrink fast: to about half after six matches, and to about a quarter after twenty. After that, how often you play matters. Each match you played in the last two weeks, beyond four, makes the next change about 3% smaller. So if you play four times a week, a 6-4 moves you about 15% less than it moves someone who plays once a week. I used to think players above about 5.8 also moved more. With far more data from strong players, that mostly went away.

## Does it pay to play up?

This is the question I care about most, because playing with better people is how I got better. Here's a 6-4 set against different opponents, for a player with a few dozen matches who plays a few times a week:

| Your team vs theirs | Win 6-4 | Lose 4-6 | Lose 6-7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 lower | +0.09 | +0.002 | +0.02 |
| 0.5 lower | +0.07 | -0.02 | -0.01 |
| Even | +0.05 | -0.05 | -0.03 |
| 0.5 higher | +0.02 | -0.07 | -0.06 |
| 1.0 higher | -0.002 | -0.09 | -0.08 |

That's for two partners at the same level. A 4-6 loss to a team 1.0 better costs you nothing, and a 6-7 goes up. Beating a much weaker team can even cost you. And from point 3, it's even better to be the weaker partner on a strong team. So playing up is close to free for your rating, and it's the best way to improve anyway.

## How I figured it out

WPR has an API behind its app, so I pulled every match for me and the five people I play with most: 272 matches, each with every player's rating before and after. Later I added full histories for eight players rated 4 to 6.7, so the model wasn't only fit on people at my level.

Then I tried formulas and checked how much of the real rating changes each one explained, always on matches it hadn't seen:

| Formula | Explained |
|---|---|
| Plain Elo: win or lose, one step size | 46% |
| Count games won, step gets smaller with experience | 83% |
| Best of three counts more | 89% |
| New players get bigger wins than losses | 93% |

The first jump was the big one. Once I switched from wins to games, most of it fell into place.

That first dataset had a blind spot. The six of us mostly played with partners near our own level, so it couldn't tell "team average" apart from "your own rating". In October I pulled a lot more: everyone I'd played with or against, then everyone they'd played with or against. That's 260 players' full histories and about 7,000 rating changes, a third of them with partners more than a full point apart. With that, the gap showed up clearly. Partners on the same team, same result, and the stronger one moved about 0.02 less per point of difference, win or lose. Letting your own rating count 70% cut the model's error by 17%.

Two weeks later I noticed the calculator was overshooting my own changes by about a fifth. So I pulled the full histories of 61 people I'd played with or against recently. On their matches from the past two weeks, which the model had never seen, it still explained about 92% of the changes, so nothing had broken. But people who played a lot moved less. Adding "matches in the last two weeks" cut the error on those new matches by another 16%, and for my own matches it halved it.

## Where it's wrong

On matches it hadn't seen, it explains about 90% of rating changes. On eight players rated 4 to 6.7 that I kept as a separate check, about 85%. WPR also keeps a hidden confidence score for each player that the API doesn't show. Some players move about 20% more or less than the calculator says, match after match, and nothing in the public data explains it. If you haven't played in a while, or you're brand new, your rating can move differently from what the calculator says.

It's still close enough to answer the question that started all this. My 6-4 in July was against a team about 0.6 below us, and a 6-4 was just about what we were supposed to do.
